The two counts are different measurements, not two answers to one question. The open-water count needs water around a bright object and rejects blobs merged with structures, so a vessel moored against a jetty falls outside it by construction. Berths are read by a second method, against each pixel's own normal appearance over earlier passes where enough have accumulated, and against a single reference frame where they have not — the berth table below says which. Neither number includes the other.

At the berths the primary figure is metres of hull alongside — 649 m on this pass — rather than a count of ships. Two vessels moored side by side or end to end merge into one patch at this resolution, so a count is a guess about where the boundary inside the patch lies, while the metres are a measurement that does not depend on that guess.

A vessel is a few bright pixels on dark water. At 9 m per pixel anything shorter than about 36 m is not counted reliably: tugs, pilot boats and barges come and go unseen. An LNG carrier is 290-345 m and is never missed, but length alone does not tell a carrier from a bulker — what the berth is for does.

None of this uses AIS. The count comes from the image, which is why it still works in a region where transponders are switched off or spoofed. The price of that independence: the image shows that a vessel is there, never its name, its cargo or which way the cargo is moving.

Berths: who is alongside

First what the image shows, then what AIS says. The two are independent, and in this region only the first can be trusted unconditionally.

From the image (independent of AIS). Read from the satellite image itself: the deck of a moored vessel reflects near-infrared light, the water next to it does not. Static structures are removed by comparing with a single reference frame of the same berth. This is the weaker of the two methods available: one reference day can itself be untypical — haze, or a vessel alongside on that very day. The per-pixel norm takes over once enough passes of each berth have accumulated. This works when AIS does not.

Under way or alongside. The colour channels of the image are recorded about a second apart, so a vessel that was moving leaves a colour trail and is excluded from the metres alongside — a hull passing the quay at speed would otherwise be logged as time at the berth, which is a loading that never happened. The limit is speed, not size: below roughly 18 km/h (about 10 knots) the displacement falls under the channel alignment error. So a trail proves the vessel was under way, while the absence of one does not prove it was stationary — a carrier easing onto a berth at 5 knots shows no trail at all.

AIS data in the Gulf is unavailable or unreliable
Since February 2026 the Strait of Hormuz and the wider Gulf have seen sustained jamming and spoofing of satellite navigation. Ships broadcast false positions and strait transits that never happened, and maritime monitoring centres warn that navigation data in the area cannot be trusted. We therefore count vessels on the satellite image instead: an image cannot be spoofed.

What stops this from being a guess

You are being asked to move people and equipment on the strength of these readings, so here is where the readings are weakest and what has been done about it. Six changes, each of which you can argue with.

Every pixel has its own empty state. A pier, a crane and a trestle are brighter than water on every image, so a brightness threshold has to be set high near the quay or the quay itself reads as a ship. We no longer ask whether a spot is brighter than its surroundings today. We ask whether it is brighter than it ever is on an empty day, pixel by pixel, across every pass in the archive. The crane stays bright and is remembered as a crane. A hull has to exceed the pixel's own normal, which works the same on open water and against a concrete wall. Before this, one reference day was subtracted; if that day happened to have a ship alongside, or haze, the error went into every later reading.

Metres of hull alongside, not a count of ships. Two vessels moored side by side or end to end merge into one patch at this resolution. Saying there are two is a guess about where the boundary inside the patch lies. Saying there are 320 metres of hull is a measurement, and it does not change with the guess. The metres are now the primary figure and the count is reported as an estimate.

A broken pass is quarantined, not reported as a quiet day. An image can be formally usable and still show nothing: thin haze, sun glint, a geometry error. It then yields zero vessels and looks like a quiet day. This is the worst kind of error in this work, because it does not break the report, it quietly falsifies it. Any pass that finds under 40% of what its neighbouring passes find is now set aside automatically. A port does not empty between two passes; an image spoils in one. Quarantined days appear as gaps, never as zeroes.

Level periods, not yesterday against today. A single day's figure is a third weather. What matters is when the level changed and whether the change held. The series is now split into periods of constant level, with the cost of declaring a change scaled to how much the series shakes on its own: a shift smaller than the jitter is not called an event. The output is of the form "the level rose from 60 to 170 MW on 12 September and has held for 54 measurements".

Movement read from a single image. The colour channels of a satellite image are not recorded at the same instant: roughly a second separates the first from the last. A stationary object has not moved and its channels coincide. A moving one leaves a trail of colour-separated ghosts, and the spacing gives speed while the order gives the axis of travel. Two consequences. Lorries on the haul roads become visible, and plant does not arrive before the lorries do, so this is the earliest evidence of work starting that open data carries. And a vessel passing the quay at speed is no longer logged as time alongside, which previously meant a loading that never happened.

We check our own speed against what ships broadcast. Any speed derived from channel timing rests on two numbers we did not measure: the interval between channels and its sign. Road traffic cannot settle them, because a lorry does not report its speed. A ship does: its transponder gives speed and course, in the same image, on every pass. So each pass carries its own accuracy check at no cost. Until enough pairs have accumulated, the axis of travel is published and the speed is not, and the direction along that axis is left open rather than guessed.

Method, and the limits we state openly

Optical satellite imagery. A usable pass every few days at 10 metres per pixel. Running equipment and flares glow brightly in the short-wave infrared, which is what allows individual trains to be told apart.

Thermal anomaly data. Several passes a day, with radiative power in megawatts. At 375 m resolution it cannot separate individual trains, but it does show the overall level of flaring.

Vessel transponder data. Arrivals, departures, time alongside, declared destination.

A vessel cannot be tied to a train. LNG from every train goes into shared storage and out over shared berths. The link is lost inside the plant. Nobody does this, paid terminals included. The attribution runs as far as berth and venture.

A ship at a quay does not show which way the cargo moves. Ras Laffan both receives project equipment and exports sulphur, and demolition debris leaves the same quays. On the image all of these are a hull alongside. We call it equipment arriving only when something else says so: AIS arrival and departure records, or a laydown yard that is visibly filling. Otherwise the direction is reported as unknown.

Inbound cargo does not identify the project. The train repair, the Barzan restoration, Pearl GTL maintenance and the North Field East expansion all run at once. Project cargo may belong to any of them.

A parked lorry is invisible; a moving one is not. A lorry covers well under one pixel, so it cannot be seen as a shape. It can still be seen as motion: the colour channels of the image are recorded about a second apart, and anything crossing the ground in that second leaves a colour-separated trail. That is a different kind of evidence from a shape, and it works below the pixel. The floor is speed, not size: below roughly 10 knots on a berth cutout, and 16 on a wide frame, the displacement is smaller than the channel alignment error and we report nothing.

AIS draught is unreliable. The field is filled in by hand and is often stale. The primary loading signal is time alongside a cargo berth.

The conclusions are automatic. Ten-metre resolution gives a hypothesis, not a fact. Each train carries a confidence level, and disputed cases are checked against the image by eye.

Second opinion: is a vessel really there, are works really under way

The same two questions asked twice, by two methods that fail differently. The code counts bright pixels: reliable, but blind to meaning — it cannot tell an LNG carrier from a barge. A vision model reads the scene but will invent detail. Neither overrides the other. Where they disagree, that zone is worth opening by eye. 6 checks via claude.
